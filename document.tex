  \large Particle-Resolved Simulation, Graph-Based Model Reduction and
  Dynamic Closures for Reactor-Scale CFD--PBM Models}
\author{Revised Concept Summary}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The project will determine how the internal topology of biological aggregates
controls steady and transient oxygen supply and will translate this information
into closures for reactor-scale simulations. Particle-resolved DNS--DEM
simulations will resolve flow and oxygen transport around the individual cells
of one aggregate at a time. A graph neural network (GNN) will be used
\emph{offline} as a structure-aware teacher model: it will test which
topological information is required beyond size and porosity and will encode
that information in a low-dimensional morphology representation. The GNN will
not be evaluated for every aggregate in the final reactor simulation. Instead,
its predictions will be compressed into a lookup table, symbolic correlation
or small neural network for three quantities: equilibrium oxygen uptake, the
dynamic response time and the fraction of poorly supplied cells. These
deployable closures will be evaluated locally for the aggregate population in
a CFD--population balance model (CFD--PBM) or for parcels in an
Euler--Lagrange model. The project thereby connects cell-resolved transport
physics to reactor-scale oxygen consumption and exposure heterogeneity without
resolving individual cells in the reactor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Goal, Scope and Scientific Advance}

The central question is:

\begin{quote}
\itshape
How does aggregate topology control oxygen uptake, oxygen-response dynamics
and the fraction of oxygen-limited cells, and how can these effects be
represented economically in reactor-scale models?
\end{quote}

Current coarse models usually treat a biological aggregate as an equivalent
homogeneous sphere. Its uptake is then calculated from size, bulk oxygen
concentration and an empirical mass-transfer or effectiveness correlation.
This can miss differences between aggregates that have the same size and
porosity but different cell connectivity, shielding, surface accessibility and
internal transport pathways.

The project will deliver three morphology-aware closures:
\begin{align}
  \eta_A &=
  F_{\eta}(D_A,\lambda_A,\Pe_A,\Da_A), \label{eq:eta_closure}\\
  \tau_A &=
  F_{\tau}(D_A,\lambda_A,\Pe_A,\Da_A), \label{eq:tau_closure}\\
  f_{\mathrm{poor},A} &=
  F_f(D_A,\lambda_A,\Pe_A,\Da_A,\chi_A), \label{eq:f_closure}
\end{align}
where \(D_A\) is aggregate size, \(\lambda_A\) is a reduced morphology
descriptor or class, \(\Pe_A\) and \(\Da_A\) describe transport and reaction,
and \(\chi_A\) is the reduced internal oxygen state. The effectiveness
\(\eta_A\) determines equilibrium uptake, \(\tau_A\) determines the memory of
the internal oxygen state, and \(f_{\mathrm{poor},A}\) quantifies cellular
exposure heterogeneity.

The resolved simulations concern isolated aggregates under controlled local
conditions. They do not directly predict the spatial distribution of
oxygen-limited cells in a reactor. Their role is to supply local constitutive
relations. At reactor scale, the CFD solution provides the oxygen concentration
and hydrodynamic conditions at the position of every population class or
parcel. In this document, \(C_{\mathrm{bulk}}\) denotes this \emph{local
unresolved carrier-phase concentration seen by the aggregate}; it is not the
spatially averaged oxygen concentration of the entire reactor. The PBM or
Euler--Lagrange model provides the local aggregate population. Evaluating the
closures for all local classes or parcels then yields the reactor-wide
distribution of uptake and oxygen-limited cell fractions.

\section{State of the Art and Identified Gap}

\subsection{Continuum diffusion--reaction models}

Established biofilm and granule models describe dissolved substrates through
continuum diffusion--reaction balances coupled to microbial kinetics. The
classical multispecies biofilm framework of Wanner and Gujer accounts for
substrate gradients, external mass-transfer resistance and biomass
development~\cite{WannerGujer1986}. For suspended granules or pellets, the
aggregate is commonly idealised as a homogeneous sphere with radial symmetry,
an effective diffusivity and Monod-type reaction kinetics. The resulting
one-dimensional problem predicts oxygen penetration depth, active-layer
thickness or an effectiveness factor as functions of aggregate radius,
effective diffusivity and kinetic parameters~\cite{Stewart2003,Wu2008}.

These models are computationally efficient and remain the appropriate
baseline. Diffusion is often the dominant internal transport mechanism, and a
recent experimental and modelling study found that heterogeneous diffusivity
had only a minor effect on the total flux for the investigated aerobic
granules~\cite{VandenBerg2020}. Consequently, the proposed project does not
presuppose that a graph model is always necessary. It tests when the
homogeneous-sphere approximation is sufficient and when it fails.

\subsection{Evidence that structure can affect oxygen transport}

Bioaggregates are not necessarily homogeneous continua. Microelectrode and
imaging studies have shown dense cell clusters, voids, channels and spatially
heterogeneous activity. De Beer et al.\ demonstrated that three-dimensional
biofilm structure altered oxygen gradients and effective exchange
area~\cite{DeBeer1994}. Transient measurements in aerobic granules further
showed heterogeneous cell and extracellular-polymer distributions and
structure-dependent oxygen response~\cite{Chiu2007}. Reconstructed-geometry
CFD studies subsequently connected granule structure with local
hydrodynamics, penetration and reaction, obtaining information that is lost
when the granule is treated as homogeneous~\cite{Liu2010}. A percolation model
has likewise indicated that connected internal pores can enhance intragranular
transport~\cite{Liu2011Percolation}.

At the other end of the modelling spectrum, individual- and particle-based
biofilm models resolve cells or biomass particles and couple them to
diffusion--reaction fields~\cite{Kreft2001,Picioreanu2004}. Such models can
represent structural heterogeneity, but they are too expensive to evaluate for
the complete aggregate population of a reactor-scale CFD calculation.

\subsection{Data-driven structure--property reduction}

Machine learning has been used to infer effective flow and transport
properties of porous structures from geometrical descriptors or resolved
images. For example, CFD-generated data have been mapped to permeability and
filtration properties using fully connected and convolutional neural
networks~\cite{Marcato2022}. Graph networks are particularly suitable when the
system is naturally represented by interacting particles or connected pores,
because message passing preserves neighbourhood and connectivity
information~\cite{SanchezGonzalez2020}.

The unresolved gap is therefore not the calculation of oxygen diffusion in an
idealised sphere, nor the cell-resolved simulation of one structure. It is the
derivation of a \emph{transferable and inexpensive closure} that retains only
the topological information demonstrably needed for reactor-scale prediction.
The novelty of the proposed project lies in combining particle-resolved
flow--oxygen simulations, graph-based structural discovery and explicit
distillation into CFD--PBM-compatible closures.

\section{Hypotheses and Work-Package Logic}

The scientific hypotheses are aligned directly with the work packages:

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
  \item[H1: Steady structure--transport relationship.]
  Aggregates with equal size and porosity can have different equilibrium
  uptake and poorly supplied cell fractions because topology changes shielding,
  accessibility and transport-path connectivity. WP2 tests H1.

  \item[H2: Morphology-dependent oxygen memory.]
  The response to changing external oxygen and flow conditions is not always
  instantaneous; its characteristic time depends on morphology and the
  competition among convection, diffusion and consumption. WP3 tests H2.

  \item[H3: Reducible topological information.]
  The structural information needed for H1 and H2 can be compressed into a
  small number of interpretable descriptors or morphology classes and used in
  inexpensive coarse-scale closures. WP4 tests H3.
\end{description}

WP1 is the enabling package that establishes the validated numerical framework
and morphology library. Figure~\ref{fig:wpflow} shows the project logic.

\section{Unified Physical and Numerical Framework}

To avoid an unnecessarily large parameter space, the baseline system is
defined once in Table~\ref{tab:framework}. Approximately \(N_A=200\) spherical
primary particles provide a useful starting point, but preliminary scaling and
grid-cost tests will determine whether a range such as
\(N_A\in\{50,200,500\}\) is needed. The key design requirement is not a
particular particle number; it is a morphology library containing structures
with similar \(D_A\) and porosity but different topology.

\subsection{Role of adhesion and distinction from the breakup project}

Adhesion is necessary to create a physically meaningful contact network, but
it is not a closure output in this project. The reference simulations use
fixed structures so that the effect of topology on oxygen transport is
identifiable. This choice separates the project from the running DFG project
on fluid-induced breakup, which predicts breakup onset, mechanism and daughter
sizes.

Restructuring and fragmentation need not be ignored. They can enter through a
\emph{one-way interface}: representative pre- and post-restructuring or
fragment geometries are treated as members of the morphology library, and the
new project quantifies their transport consequences. Thus the proposal can
show that mechanics affects oxygen supply without duplicating the development
of a breakup model. A later coarse reactor model may obtain morphological
transitions from experiments, a PBM kernel or the existing breakup project.

\subsection{Resolved flow and oxygen equations}

The liquid is described by the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations. An
immersed-boundary or fictitious-domain formulation resolves the no-slip
surfaces of all primary cells. Dissolved oxygen satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial C}{\partial t}
  +\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla C
  =D_{\Otwo}\nabla^2C+S_C.
  \label{eq:oxygen}
\end{equation}
At the surface \(S_i\) of cell \(i\),
\begin{equation}
  -D_{\Otwo}\nabla C\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=j_i,
  \qquad
  j_i=k_s C_{s,i},
  \label{eq:first_order}
\end{equation}
for the first-order baseline. A subsequent biological case uses
\begin{equation}
  j_i=j_{\max}\frac{C_{s,i}}{K_{\Otwo}+C_{s,i}}.
  \label{eq:monod}
\end{equation}
The surface-averaged concentration and uptake of each cell are
\begin{equation}
  C_i(t)=\frac{1}{A_i}\int_{S_i}C\,\diff A,
  \qquad
  Q_i(t)=\int_{S_i}j_i\,\diff A,
  \qquad
  Q_A(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_A}Q_i(t).
  \label{eq:cell_outputs}
\end{equation}
These cell-level labels make it possible to compute total uptake and the
internal distribution of oxygen exposure from the same simulation.

\subsection{Dimensionless design space}

The P\'eclet number measures convection relative to molecular diffusion:
\begin{equation}
  \Pe_A=\frac{U_A D_A}{D_{\Otwo}},
  \qquad
  \Pe_{\dot{\gamma}}=\frac{\dot{\gamma}D_A^2}{D_{\Otwo}}
  \quad\text{for shear-dominated conditions}.
  \label{eq:pe}
\end{equation}
It determines the external boundary layer and the extent to which flow can
refresh internal pore spaces. The Damk\"ohler number measures consumption
relative to diffusive supply. For first-order uptake,
\begin{equation}
  \Da_A=\frac{k_s A_{\mathrm{tot}}}{D_{\Otwo}D_A}.
  \label{eq:da}
\end{equation}
Large \(\Da_A\) favours strong internal depletion; large \(\Pe_A\) can improve
external and pore-scale supply, depending on topology. Expressing the database
in terms of \(\Pe_A\) and \(\Da_A\) reduces dependence on one specific reactor
or organism and makes the resulting closures transferable.

\subsection{Validation and turbulence simulations}

Validation proceeds from diffusion/reaction and forced convection around one
sphere to regular clusters and then to uniform flow and simple shear.
Conservation of total reactive flux, grid convergence, symmetry and available
\(\Sh\) limits are verified before HIT simulations.

The main database contains one structurally fixed aggregate per
particle-resolved HIT simulation. A scalar-forcing term maintains a prescribed
domain-mean bulk concentration despite oxygen consumption. Statistically
stationary cases determine equilibrium quantities. Step, pulse and periodic
changes of bulk oxygen, supplemented by a small number of reactor-derived
histories, determine dynamic response. Different simulations represent the
range of local conditions encountered along reactor trajectories; the
reactor-scale population model later combines these local responses.

\section{Graph Representation and Offline Learning}

For aggregate \(r\), the graph
\(\mathcal{G}_r=(\mathcal{V}_r,\mathcal{E}_r)\) contains one node per primary
cell. Compact static node features describe radius, relative position,
coordination, local void fraction, distance from the aggregate surface and
accessible surface area. Two edge definitions are compared: physical contacts
and spatial neighbours within a cutoff. Edge features describe relative
position, gap/contact state and a local shielding or pore-throat measure.
Global operating conditions such as \(\Pe_A\), \(\Da_A\) and
\(C_{\mathrm{bulk}}\) are supplied separately.

The graph describes morphology; the resolved CFD fields generate targets.
Dynamic variables such as \(C_i(t)\) and \(Q_i(t)\) are therefore primarily
labels for learning and physical analysis, not indispensable inputs to the
static graph encoder. This avoids hiding the answer inside the graph features.

\subsection{GNN implementation in PyTorch Geometric}

The GNN will be implemented in Python using \emph{PyTorch Geometric} (PyG), a
PyTorch-based framework for learning on graphs, point clouds and other
irregular data structures \cite{FeyLenssen2019}. Each aggregate is stored as
one graph object with
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{G}_A=
  \bigl(\boldsymbol{X}_A,\boldsymbol{E}_A,
  \boldsymbol{A}_A,\boldsymbol{u}_A,\boldsymbol{y}_A\bigr),
  \label{eq:pyg_data}
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{X}_A\) contains particle/node features,
\(\boldsymbol{E}_A\) identifies the contact or neighbourhood edges,
\(\boldsymbol{A}_A\) contains edge features, \(\boldsymbol{u}_A\) contains
the separately supplied aggregate and operating variables, and
\(\boldsymbol{y}_A=[\eta_A,\tau_A,f_{\mathrm{poor},A}]\) contains the resolved
targets. Graphs with different particle numbers can be combined in mini-batches
without padding them to one fixed size.

The initial architecture comprises three or four message-passing layers with
edge features, followed by permutation-invariant global pooling. The pooled
vector is the aggregate representation \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_A\). It is
concatenated with \(D_A\), \(N_A\), \(\Pe_A\), \(\Da_A\) and the local oxygen
condition and passed to a small graph-level regressor. Edge-aware layers such
as GINE are the primary option because relative distance, contact state and
shielding are expected to matter; simpler graph-convolution and attention
models provide baselines.

This computational strategy has precedent in related physical systems.
Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. represented particles as nodes and local interactions
as edges to learn the dynamics of fluids, rigid solids and deformable
materials \cite{SanchezGonzalez2020}. Graph representations have also been
used to infer transport properties of pore networks from high-fidelity flow
simulations \cite{IAnson2025}. These studies support graph-based learning for
particle and transport systems, while the proposed morphology-aware oxygen
closures for bioaggregates constitute the application-specific development.

\subsection{Why use a GNN if the final closure is simple?}

The GNN is an \emph{offline discovery and teacher model}. It answers whether
topology contains predictive information beyond hand-selected descriptors and
which parts of the contact/neighbourhood network matter. For each resolved
aggregate,
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{z}_r=\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}_r),
  \qquad
  \widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}_r=
  \mathcal{H}_{\theta}
  (\boldsymbol{z}_r,D_{A,r},\Pe_{A,r},\Da_{A,r}),
  \label{eq:gnn_teacher}
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{y}\) contains \(\eta_A\), \(\tau_A\) and
\(f_{\mathrm{poor},A}\), or the relevant subset for a given experiment.
Attention/sensitivity analyses and comparison with conventional descriptors
are used to interpret \(\boldsymbol{z}_r\).

\subsection{Separation of morphology and operating conditions}

The graph encoder must describe the aggregate structure only. Therefore, for a
fixed aggregate \(i\),
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,i}
  =\mathcal{E}_{\theta}(\mathcal{G}_{A,i})
  \label{eq:morphology_encoder}
\end{equation}
is independent of the flow condition. The local operating conditions are
introduced only in the subsequent prediction network:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\boldsymbol y}_{ij}
  =\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
  \left(
  \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,i},D_{A,i},N_{A,i},
  \Pe_{ij},\Da_{ij},C_{\infty,ij}
  \right),
  \label{eq:condition_decoder}
\end{equation}
where \(j\) identifies the operating condition and
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol y_{ij}
  =
  \left[
  \eta_{A,ij},\tau_{A,ij},f_{\mathrm{poor},A,ij}
  \right].
\end{equation}
This architecture prevents flow information from being mixed into the
morphology descriptor.

Consider ten fixed aggregate structures, each simulated under six different
flow conditions. The resolved database then contains
\begin{equation}
  10\ \text{structures}\times6\ \text{conditions}
  =60\ \text{labelled simulation cases}.
\end{equation}
However, the graph encoder produces only
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{10\ \text{unique morphology vectors}}
\end{equation}
because each fixed graph is encoded once. The same vector
\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,i}\) is combined with six different sets of
operating inputs:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,i}^{(1)}
  =\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,i}^{(2)}
  =\cdots
  =\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,i}^{(6)}.
\end{equation}
The six simulations teach the prediction network how one morphology responds
to different environments; they do not define six different morphologies.

More generally, for \(N_G\) fixed graphs and \(N_C\) operating conditions, the
database contains \(N_GN_C\) labelled cases but only \(N_G\) unique morphology
vectors. A separate morphology vector for each condition is appropriate only
if flow causes deformation, restructuring or breakup, so that the underlying
graph itself changes.

The complete graph and GNN are unavailable for every PBM class or parcel in a
reactor. WP4 therefore compresses the teacher into a deployable morphology
coordinate. This reduction is not an arbitrary assignment; it is constructed
and validated in four explicit steps.

\subsection{Derivation of an interpretable morphology coordinate}

\paragraph{Step 1: construct candidate physical descriptors.}
For every aggregate in the resolved library, conventional and graph-derived
descriptors are calculated before training. Candidate quantities include
porosity \(\phi_A\), mean coordination number \(\bar Z_A\), anisotropy
\(a_A\), accessible surface fraction \(s_{\mathrm{acc},A}\), mean shortest
path from an internal cell to an accessible surface
\(\ell_{\mathrm{surf},A}\), and transport-network measures such as the size of
the connected pore or neighbourhood component. They form
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{d}_A=
  \left[
  \phi_A,\bar Z_A,a_A,s_{\mathrm{acc},A},
  \ell_{\mathrm{surf},A},\ldots
  \right].
  \label{eq:descriptor_vector}
\end{equation}
The set is intentionally compact and contains quantities that can ultimately
be extracted from imaging, aggregate-generation simulations or empirical
morphology distributions.

\paragraph{Step 2: discover missing structural information with the GNN.}
The GNN embedding \(\boldsymbol{z}_A\) is compared with
\(\boldsymbol{d}_A\). Ablation tests answer whether size and porosity alone,
then the full conventional descriptor vector, reproduce the resolved closure
targets. The GNN is retained only if it reduces the prediction error on
aggregates that were not used for training. Sensitivity analysis identifies
which nodes, edges and descriptors drive this improvement.

\paragraph{Step 3: define the reduced coordinate or classes.}
Two alternatives are evaluated:
\begin{align}
  \lambda_A &=g(\boldsymbol{d}_A),
  &&\text{continuous descriptor}, \label{eq:lambda_continuous}\\
  m_A &=\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{z}_A)
  \in\{\mathrm{M1},\mathrm{M2},\mathrm{M3}\},
  &&\text{discrete classes}. \label{eq:lambda_classes}
\end{align}
The mapping \(g\) can be obtained by sparse regression or symbolic regression
against the relevant GNN embedding coordinate and, more importantly, against
the resolved transport targets. A purely illustrative result could be
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_A
  \propto
  \frac{s_{\mathrm{acc},A}}
  {(1+\bar Z_A)\,\ell_{\mathrm{surf},A}},
  \label{eq:lambda_example}
\end{equation}
where a high value represents an open, accessible structure and a low value a
shielded structure with long internal paths. Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda_example}
is not assumed beforehand; its functional form and necessity are determined
from the data.

If discrete classes are more robust, clustering is performed in the validated
embedding space and the clusters are translated into physical descriptions.
An anticipated, but not prescribed, interpretation is given in
Table~\ref{tab:morphclasses}.

\paragraph{Step 4: distil and validate the deployable closure.}
The validated GNN teacher and the resolved cases are sampled over the required
\((D_A,\lambda_A,\Pe_A,\Da_A)\) domain. A low-cost student is then fitted as
either (i) a lookup table with interpolation, (ii) a symbolic correlation, or
(iii) a small fully connected neural network if the dependence is too
nonlinear for the first two forms. The student is accepted only if it remains
accurate for withheld aggregate geometries and operating conditions.

The three deployment forms are alternatives, not three sequential ML models.
The preferred order is symbolic correlation, lookup table and then small ANN,
based on the balance of interpretability, interpolation accuracy and numerical
cost. A direct small ANN based only on prescribed bulk descriptors is retained
as a baseline; the GNN is justified only if it adds demonstrable predictive
information.

Symbolic regression is therefore a genuine alternative to the final small
ANN, rather than an additional layer placed after it. Using, for example,
PySR, candidate expressions are fitted directly to the resolved/GNN-teacher
data,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{y}_k=
  F_k(D_A,N_A,\lambda_A,\Pe_A,\Da_A,C_\infty),
  \qquad
  y_k\in\{\eta_A,\tau_A,f_{\mathrm{poor},A}\},
  \label{eq:symbolic_student}
\end{equation}
while jointly considering prediction error and expression complexity. Physical
constraints can restrict the admissible operators and enforce bounds such as
\(0\leq\eta_A\leq1\) and \(0\leq f_{\mathrm{poor},A}\leq1\). This route is
especially well motivated here because Cranmer et al. demonstrated that
symbolic regression can distil trained graph networks into explicit physical
relations \cite{Cranmer2020}; PySR provides a practical open-source
implementation for scientific equation discovery \cite{Cranmer2023}. If no
compact expression reaches the prescribed validation accuracy, the small ANN
is retained as the deployable closure.

\subsection{Dynamic model and conditional role of Neural ODEs}

The first dynamic model is deliberately simple. The aggregate carries a
reduced oxygen or uptake state \(\chi_A\):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\diff\chi_A}{\diff t}
  =\frac{\chi_{A,\mathrm{eq}}
  (C_{\mathrm{bulk}},\Pe_A,\Da_A,\lambda_A)-\chi_A}
  {\tau_A(D_A,\lambda_A,\Pe_A,\Da_A)}.
  \label{eq:relaxation}
\end{equation}
The response time is obtained from resolved step responses, for example using
the integral of the normalised relaxation curve. This one-state equation is
the primary deliverable because it is interpretable and inexpensive.

Only if step, pulse and periodic tests show systematic multi-time-scale or
hysteretic behaviour will a graph-conditioned Neural ODE be investigated:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\diff\boldsymbol{\chi}_A}{\diff t}
  =\boldsymbol{F}_{\theta}
  (\boldsymbol{\chi}_A,C_{\mathrm{bulk}},\Pe_A,\Da_A,\lambda_A).
  \label{eq:node}
\end{equation}
The Neural ODE does not automatically become a lookup table. It is either
retained as a small dynamic operator or distilled into the lowest-order
multi-state ODE that reproduces the resolved histories. This decision is made
from validation evidence, not fixed in advance.

\section{Closure Definitions and Use in the Coarse Model}

\subsection{Equilibrium uptake}

For a known biological kinetic law evaluated at local bulk oxygen,
\begin{equation}
  Q_{\mathrm{kin}}
  =N_A q(C_{\mathrm{bulk}}),
  \qquad
  Q_{A,\mathrm{eq}}=\eta_A Q_{\mathrm{kin}}.
  \label{eq:uptake}
\end{equation}
For example,
\begin{equation}
  q(C_{\mathrm{bulk}})
  =q_{\max}\frac{C_{\mathrm{bulk}}}
  {K_{\Otwo}+C_{\mathrm{bulk}}}.
\end{equation}
The closure in Eq.~\eqref{eq:eta_closure} corrects the hypothetical
fully supplied rate for transport limitations caused by boundary layers,
shielding and internal depletion.

\subsection{Poorly supplied cell fraction}

For a defined biological threshold \(C_{\mathrm{crit}}\), the resolved target is
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{poor},A}(t)
  =\frac{1}{N_A}\sum_{i=1}^{N_A}
  \mathbb{I}\!\left[C_i(t)<C_{\mathrm{crit}}\right].
  \label{eq:fpoor}
\end{equation}
At equilibrium this is a direct closure of morphology and local conditions.
Under transients, it additionally depends on the carried internal state
\(\chi_A\). It is not an oxygen sink by itself; it is an exposure indicator
available to models of growth, productivity, viability or metabolic state.

\subsection{Local quantities seen by a PBM class or parcel}

For an Euler--Lagrange parcel \(p\), the oxygen concentration supplied to the
closure is interpolated from the carrier-phase field:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\infty,p}(t)
  =
  C\!\left(\boldsymbol{x}_p(t),t\right).
  \label{eq:local_oxygen}
\end{equation}
This is the coarse-grid concentration surrounding the unresolved aggregate. It
plays the role of \(C_{\mathrm{bulk}}\) in the particle-resolved training case,
but it is neither a reactor average nor the unresolved concentration inside
the aggregate. The local relative velocity and shear magnitude are, for
example,
\begin{align}
  U_{\mathrm{rel},p}
  &=
  \left|
  \boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_p,t)-\boldsymbol{v}_p
  \right|,\\
  \dot{\gamma}_p
  &=
  \sqrt{2\boldsymbol{S}:\boldsymbol{S}}\,
  \big|_{\boldsymbol{x}_p},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{S}
  =
  \tfrac{1}{2}
  \left(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\right).
\end{align}
Accordingly, the relevant P\'eclet number can be based on slip or shear:
\begin{equation}
  \Pe_{\mathrm{slip},p}
  =\frac{U_{\mathrm{rel},p}D_{A,p}}{D_{\Otwo}},
  \qquad
  \Pe_{\mathrm{shear},p}
  =\frac{\dot{\gamma}_pD_{A,p}^{2}}{D_{\Otwo}}.
  \label{eq:local_pe}
\end{equation}
The closure development determines whether one of these definitions or both
are required. An Eulerian CFD--PBM uses the corresponding local CFD values for
each size--morphology class in a control volume.

The final online closure therefore combines a morphology property carried by
the parcel with aggregate properties and locally evaluated reactor
conditions:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \begin{bmatrix}
  \eta_{A,p}\\[1mm]
  \tau_{A,p}\\[1mm]
  f_{\mathrm{poor},A,p}
  \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \boldsymbol F
  \left(
  D_{A,p},N_{A,p},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,p},
  \Pe_p,\Da_p,\chi_p
  \right)}.
  \label{eq:online_closure_complete}
\end{equation}
Here \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,p}\) originates from the offline graph analysis,
whereas \(\Pe_p\), \(\Da_p\) and the surrounding oxygen concentration are
calculated from the local reactor state. In an Euler--Lagrange implementation,
the fluid quantities are interpolated at the parcel position
\(\boldsymbol{x}_p\), representing the centre of the unresolved aggregate.
They are not values inside a particle-resolved sphere. For aggregates that are
large relative to the CFD grid, a kernel-weighted average over neighbouring
cells may be used instead of single-point interpolation.

The online reactor model does not evaluate the complete GNN. It stores only
\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,p}\), assigned at parcel creation from measured
descriptors, an aggregate-generation model or a prescribed conditional
distribution. The complete separation is
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{
  \mathcal{G}_{A,i}\longrightarrow
  \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{A,i}}
  _{\text{offline graph encoder}}
  \qquad
  \underbrace{
  (D_A,N_A,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_A,\Pe_A,\Da_A,\chi_A)
  \longrightarrow
  (\eta_A,\tau_A,f_{\mathrm{poor},A})}
  _{\text{online low-cost closure}}.
  \label{eq:offline_online_separation}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Online calculation for one PBM class or parcel}

The final model performs the following inexpensive calculation at each reactor
location and time:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Interpolate the local carrier-phase oxygen concentration
  \(C_{\infty,p}\), velocity gradients, turbulence quantities and fluid
  properties from CFD.
  \item Read class/parcel properties \(D_A\), \(N_A\) and \(\lambda_A\).
  \item Calculate \(\Pe_A\) and \(\Da_A\).
  \item Query the selected lookup table, symbolic correlation or small ANN for
  \(\eta_A\), \(\tau_A\) and \(f_{\mathrm{poor},A}\).
  \item Calculate \(Q_{A,\mathrm{eq}}=\eta_A N_Aq(C_{\infty,p})\), update
  the dynamic state with Eq.~\eqref{eq:relaxation}, and obtain current
  \(Q_A\) and \(f_{\mathrm{poor},A}\).
\end{enumerate}

For a morphology-resolved PBM,
\begin{equation}
  n=n(D_A,\lambda_A,\boldsymbol{x},t),
  \qquad
  S_{\Otwo}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
  =-\sum_{k,m}n_{k,m}Q_{A,k,m}.
  \label{eq:pbm_sink}
\end{equation}
The same logic applies to an Euler--Lagrange parcel with statistical weight
\(w_p\), whose sink contribution is \(-w_pQ_{A,p}\). The local number or
fraction of poorly supplied cells is reconstructed by weighting
\(f_{\mathrm{poor},k,m}\) with \(N_{A,k}n_{k,m}\), or the corresponding parcel
weights. The reactor distribution therefore emerges from many local
evaluations of single-aggregate closures, not from placing individual cells in
the reactor mesh.

If morphology is resolved only by discrete classes, the PBM carries a small
number of size--morphology populations. If only a measured conditional
morphology distribution \(P(\lambda_A\mid D_A)\) is available, the closures can
be averaged over that distribution, with a documented loss of
morphology-specific information.

\subsection{How morphology is assigned in the reactor model}

The transport closure cannot infer the morphology of an unresolved reactor
parcel from local oxygen and shear alone. A morphology coordinate must
therefore enter as a parcel property or PBM internal coordinate. Three levels
of implementation are distinguished.

\paragraph{Level 1: prescribed morphology distribution.}
For the first reactor demonstrator, a parcel receives its morphology at
injection or creation from a measured or simulated conditional distribution,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_p\sim P(\lambda_A\mid D_{A,p}),
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  m_p\sim P(m_A\mid D_{A,p}).
  \label{eq:morph_sampling}
\end{equation}
It then carries
\begin{equation}
  \left\{
  D_{A,p},N_{A,p},\lambda_p,\chi_p,w_p
  \right\},
  \label{eq:parcel_state}
\end{equation}
and \(\lambda_p\) remains fixed along the trajectory. This is the reference
assumption for demonstrating the oxygen closures without introducing an
unvalidated morphology-evolution model.

\paragraph{Level 2: descriptor-based assignment.}
If imaging or an aggregate-generation model supplies porosity, coordination,
anisotropy and accessibility, Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda_continuous} is evaluated
directly when the parcel is created. The coarse simulation stores only
\(\lambda_p\), not the complete graph.

\paragraph{Level 3: morphology-resolved population dynamics.}
An extended PBM uses \(n(D_A,\lambda_A,\boldsymbol{x},t)\) and includes
transition kernels for aggregation, growth, restructuring or breakup. These
kernels determine how a new or transformed aggregate enters another
morphology class. Their derivation is not part of the core oxygen-transport
project. They may be supplied by experiments, aggregate-generation
simulations or the existing breakup project.

\subsection{Explicit assumptions of the first coarse-scale demonstrator}

The proposed first implementation therefore assumes that:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item every parcel represents aggregates with one size and one reduced
  morphology value or class;
  \item the morphology distribution at parcel creation is known or prescribed;
  \item morphology remains fixed during a parcel trajectory unless an external
  transition model is supplied;
  \item the local CFD concentration \(C_{\infty,p}\) is resolved on the reactor
  mesh, whereas internal aggregate oxygen is represented only by \(\chi_p\);
  \item the oxygen closure predicts uptake and exposure, not aggregate growth
  or morphology evolution;
  \item a separate biological law is required to convert oxygen exposure into
  a PBM growth rate.
\end{enumerate}

These assumptions make the final use of the model unambiguous. The core
deliverable is a morphology-conditioned oxygen model. A morphology-evolution
model and an oxygen-dependent growth kernel are downstream couplings, not
quantities silently inferred by the GNN.

\section{Work Packages, Milestones and Outputs}

\subsection*{WP1: Resolved framework, validation and morphology library}

WP1 defines a reference biological system, implements conservative reactive
transport in the particle-resolved solver and validates single particles,
regular clusters and canonical flows. It constructs the controlled morphology
library and establishes the feasible ranges of \(N_A\), \(\Pe_A\) and
\(\Da_A\). \textbf{Milestone M1:} verified mass conservation, grid convergence
and a database design that exhibits measurable transport limitation.

\subsection*{WP2: Steady topology-dependent transport (H1)}

WP2 performs steady uniform-flow, shear and HIT simulations across the designed
structure--transport space. Paired structures with equal size and similar
porosity isolate the effect of topology. The outputs are
\(\eta_A\), \(Q_{A,\mathrm{eq}}\), equilibrium
\(f_{\mathrm{poor},A}\), and a mechanistic analysis of shielding, accessibility
and flow penetration. \textbf{Milestone M2:} quantitative acceptance or
rejection of H1 and validated steady targets for model reduction.

\subsection*{WP3: Transient oxygen response (H2)}

WP3 applies step, pulse, periodic and selected reactor-derived oxygen histories
to representative morphologies. It determines \(\tau_A\), tests the validity
of the one-state relaxation model and introduces a higher-order ODE or Neural
ODE only when justified. \textbf{Milestone M3:} a validated dynamic model over
the relevant ratio of aggregate and reactor fluctuation time scales.

\subsection*{WP4: Graph reduction and reactor-scale integration (H3)}

WP4 trains and interprets the GNN teacher, constructs \(\lambda_A\) or discrete
morphology classes, and distils the teacher into the simplest accurate online
closure. The three closures are implemented first in an Euler--Lagrange
demonstrator and, subject to feasibility, in a morphology-resolved CFD--PBM.
\textbf{Milestone M4:} reactor-scale predictions of oxygen sink and
oxygen-limited cell fractions with quantified closure uncertainty.

The main outputs are therefore:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a validated particle-resolved solver and database for reactive
  transport in bioaggregates;
  \item physical evidence for when topology matters beyond size and porosity;
  \item closures for equilibrium effectiveness, oxygen-response time and
  poorly supplied cell fraction;
  \item an interpretable morphology representation discovered with graph-based
  learning;
  \item a verified low-cost closure implementation for CFD--PBM or
  Euler--Lagrange reactor simulation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Feasibility, Risk Control and Non-Overlap}

The decisive preliminary test is whether realistic size and uptake ranges
produce effects large enough to matter:
\begin{equation}
  \eta_A<1,\qquad
  f_{\mathrm{poor},A}>0,\qquad
  \tau_A/\tau_{\mathrm{reactor}}\not\ll1,
  \label{eq:feasibility}
\end{equation}
and whether equal-size, equal-porosity structures differ beyond numerical and
sampling uncertainty. A diffusion estimate
\(\tau_D\sim R_A^2/D_{\mathrm{eff}}\) and a small resolved pilot matrix will
determine the aggregate-size and kinetic ranges before the full database is
generated.

The project is modular if individual assumptions fail. If topology adds no
information beyond conventional descriptors, that negative result supports a
simpler closure without a morphology coordinate. If a single response time is
sufficient, the optional Neural ODE is omitted. If morphology-resolved PBM
transport is too expensive or lacks transition data, the closures are first
demonstrated with Euler--Lagrange parcels or morphology-averaged PBM classes.

Non-overlap with the running DFG project is defined by the predicted
quantities. The existing project studies mechanical breakup onset, mechanism,
fragment motion and daughter-size distributions and derives a breakup closure.
The proposed project predicts reactive mass transfer, oxygen memory and
cell-level oxygen exposure for prescribed morphologies. Shared numerical
infrastructure and selected aggregate geometries constitute enabling
preliminary work; the new simulations, labels, hypotheses and coarse-scale
closures concern oxygen transport rather than mechanical failure.

\section{Concise Project Statement}

\begin{quote}
The project will use particle-resolved DNS--DEM simulations to determine how
bioaggregate topology controls equilibrium oxygen uptake, transient oxygen
memory and the fraction of poorly supplied cells. A GNN will serve offline to
discover and encode the necessary structural information. Its knowledge will
then be distilled into one inexpensive deployable form---a symbolic
correlation, lookup table or small neural network---which is evaluated for
aggregate classes or parcels in a coarse CFD--PBM or Euler--Lagrange reactor
model. The resulting framework will predict reactor-scale oxygen demand and
the spatial distribution of oxygen-limited cellular material without resolving
individual cells in the reactor.
\end{quote}
